SCENE 1 — Hook (~30 seconds)
------------------------------
Imagine you have a two-dimensional grid — a flat sheet of graph paper stretching out in every direction. Now apply a matrix transformation. The entire grid warps: squares become parallelograms, circles become ellipses, and almost every vector gets knocked off its original direction. But here's the fascinating question: are there any special vectors that refuse to turn? Vectors that stay perfectly on their original line, only getting stretched or compressed? Those special directions are called eigenvectors, and the factors by which they stretch are called eigenvalues. Let's find them.


SCENE 2 — Concept Introduction (~50 seconds)
----------------------------------------------
A linear transformation takes any vector x and maps it to a new vector A times x, where A is our matrix. Most vectors change both their length and their direction. But an eigenvector v is different. When we multiply A times v, the result is simply lambda times v — the same direction, just scaled by a factor lambda. This is the fundamental eigen-equation: A v equals lambda v. Here, v must be non-zero; otherwise the equation is trivially true for any lambda. The scalar lambda is called the eigenvalue, and it tells us the stretch factor. If lambda is positive, the vector stretches or compresses along its line. If lambda is negative, it also flips direction. And if lambda equals zero, the vector collapses to the origin. Our learning objective: by the end of this video, the viewer will be able to explain what eigenvalues and eigenvectors represent geometrically under a linear transformation, and calculate them step by step for a two-by-two matrix.


SCENE 3 — Visual Intuition (~50 seconds)
------------------------------------------
Let's see this in action with our matrix A equals three, one, zero, two. Watch the grid as we apply the transformation. Notice how most vectors rotate — they get pushed off their original direction. I've drawn their original span lines as dotted lines so you can see the change. But look at the vector along the x-axis: one, zero. After the transformation, it lands at three, zero — exactly three times its original length, still pointing in the same direction. That's eigenvector v-one with eigenvalue three. Now look at the vector minus one, one. After the transformation, it lands at minus two, two — exactly two times its original. Same direction, just stretched. That's eigenvector v-two with eigenvalue two. These are the only two independent directions that survive the transformation unchanged. Every other vector rotates.


SCENE 4 — Worked Example (~100 seconds)
-----------------------------------------
Now let's derive these algebraically, step by step. We start with the eigen-equation: A v equals lambda v. Rearranging, we get A minus lambda I times v equals the zero vector. For a non-trivial solution — meaning v is not the zero vector — the matrix A minus lambda I must be singular. That means its determinant must equal zero. This gives us the characteristic equation. For our matrix A equals three, one, zero, two, we compute A minus lambda I as three minus lambda, one, zero, two minus lambda. The determinant is three minus lambda times two minus lambda, minus one times zero, which simplifies to three minus lambda times two minus lambda equals zero. Setting each factor to zero: lambda one equals three, and lambda two equals two. These are our eigenvalues. Now we find the eigenvectors. For lambda one equals three: A minus three I equals zero, one, zero, negative one. Setting this times v equal to zero, row reduction gives us y equals zero with x free. So the eigenvector is v-one equals one, zero — or any scalar multiple. For lambda two equals two: A minus two I equals one, one, zero, zero. Setting this times v equal to zero gives x plus y equals zero, so y equals negative x. Taking x equals negative one, the eigenvector is v-two equals negative one, one.


SCENE 5 — Interpretation and Verification (~40 seconds)
---------------------------------------------------------
Let's verify our results numerically. A times v-one: the matrix three, one, zero, two times the vector one, zero gives three, zero. And three times v-one is also three, zero. Check — they match! A times v-two: the matrix times negative one, one gives negative two, two. And two times v-two is also negative two, two. Check — perfect match! Geometrically, eigenvalue three means the x-axis direction gets stretched to three times its length. Eigenvalue two means the direction along negative one, one gets doubled. If we had a negative eigenvalue, the vector would flip through the origin while scaling. And an eigenvalue of zero would collapse the vector to the origin entirely, meaning that direction lies in the null space of A.


SCENE 6 — Summary and Credits (~30 seconds)
----------------------------------------------
Let's recap three key takeaways. First: eigenvectors are the special directions that remain unchanged — only scaled — under a linear transformation. Second: eigenvalues are found by solving the characteristic equation, det of A minus lambda I equals zero. Third: eigenvectors are found by solving the null space of A minus lambda I for each eigenvalue. Thank you for watching!
